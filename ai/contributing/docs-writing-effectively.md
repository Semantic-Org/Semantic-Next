# Writing Effectively

> **Skill:** `sui:docs-writing-effectively`
> **Purpose:** Framework for writing that changes reader understanding — problem-first structure, instability language, argument over explanation

---

## The Core Reframe

**Writing is not about communicating your ideas to readers. It is about changing their ideas.**

This single insight restructures everything. Most prose fails not because it's unclear, but because it's *useless*—it doesn't change anything for the reader.

The hierarchy of writing quality:
1. **Valuable** (does this change something for the reader?)
2. **Persuasive** (will they believe it?)
3. **Organized** (can they follow it?)
4. **Clear** (can they understand each part?)

Clear + useless = useless. Organized + useless = useless. Persuasive + useless = useless.

---

## The Interference Pattern Problem

Writers and readers process text differently:

- **Writers** generate text on a "horizontal axis"—using writing to develop and refine their own thinking
- **Readers** process on a "vertical axis"—reading to change how they see the world

When you hand readers prose optimized for your thinking process, you interfere with their reading process. They:
1. Slow down
2. Don't understand
3. Get aggravated
4. Stop

Most prose trained on educational writing inherits this interference pattern—because teachers were *paid to care about the writer*. Real-world readers read only because they think the text is *valuable to them*.

## Stability vs. Instability: The Central Error

### The Traditional Model (Stability)

Most writers are trained in a "martini glass" model:
- Open with **generalizations** or **background**
- Provide **definitions**
- State **thesis**
- Argue specifics
- Conclude with generalization

This model assumes knowledge is *cumulative and stable*—you build on a solid foundation of what came before. The language reflects this: "building on," "grounded in," "based on," "consistent with."

### What Readers Actually Want (Instability)

Readers search for *instability*—tension, problems, contradictions, things that don't fit. The language that signals value:

**Instability markers:**
- but, however, although, nonetheless, yet
- inconsistent, contradicts, challenges, anomaly
- tension, conflict, problem, gap, error

**Community markers:**
- widely accepted, reported, established
- the dominant view, conventional wisdom
- scholars have argued, research shows

If your opening paragraphs lack these words, readers cannot locate value.

## Problem → Solution, Not Background → Thesis

### The Structure That Works

1. **Open with a problem** located in something readers care about
2. **Establish instability** using tension-creating language
3. **Show costs or benefits** of the instability
4. **Position your thesis as a solution** to the problem

The problem must be the *readers' problem* (or something they care about), not yours. "Ever since I was young, I've been fascinated by X" is death—it locates the problem in the writer.

### Building Problems vs. Giving Background

These look similar but function completely differently:

**Background (stability):** "Ever since Herodotus, historians have written about events—battles, alliances, scandals..."
- Reader assumes: this is foundation-setting
- Reader expects: smooth continuation

**Problem-building (instability):** "Ever since Herodotus, historians have written about events—battles, alliances, scandals... *But* despite this prominence, the event has rarely been scrutinized as a theoretical category."
- Reader assumes: something is wrong
- Reader expects: tension, challenge, value

The word "but" (or "however," "although," etc.) transforms the preceding material from background into *setup for instability*.

## Gap vs. Error: Why "New" Fails

### The Gap Trap

Many writers use "gap" framing: "Scholars have studied X, but no one has examined Y."

This assumes a *bounded* model of knowledge—a crossword puzzle with finite pieces. Fill in a piece, and you've contributed.

But if knowledge is *infinite*, filling one gap leaves infinite gaps. You've done nothing valuable.

**Gap framing fails because:**
- "New" and "original" don't mean valuable
- Readers ask: "So what? Why should we care about this gap?"
- There's no inherent cost to leaving it unfilled

### The Error Approach

More powerful: "The dominant view holds X, but this is *wrong* because..."

This works because:
- It challenges something readers already believe
- It implies a cost—readers are operating on a false premise
- It requires *argument*, not just explanation

**The code for challenging readers politely:**
> "Your work has been brilliant. You've advanced this field enormously. *But* there's this one small thing that's wrong..."

This isn't sycophancy—it's the code. Every published article implicitly says "you're wrong about something." The code determines whether readers engage or dismiss.

## Argue, Don't Explain

### Explaining Is for Teachers

Explanation reveals the inside of your head—demonstrating that you understand something. This is what teachers wanted: show me you know the material.

No one outside school cares about the inside of your head.

### Arguing Is for Readers

Argument predicts what readers will *doubt* and addresses those doubts. It assumes readers will challenge you—because that's their professional function.

**The question to ask:**
- ❌ "Why do I think this?" (teacher's question)
- ✅ "Why should *they* think this?" (reader's question)

Explanation happens *inside* value already established. You cannot explain your way to value. If readers don't perceive a problem, your explanation has no anchor.

## Community Codes

Every community has specific words that signal value. You must learn the codes of your target community.

### Finding the Codes

McEnerney's exercise: Spend 15 minutes weekly reading articles in your field. Circle every word that creates value for readers. Build a word list. When revising, check whether your first two paragraphs contain 10+ coded words.

### Some Universal Codes

| Function | Words/Phrases |
|----------|---------------|
| Instability | but, however, although, nonetheless, yet, inconsistent, anomaly, tension, challenge |
| Community | widely accepted, established, reported, scholars argue, research shows, dominant view |
| Cost | risk, problem, failure, unable to, limits our understanding, leaves unresolved |
| Benefit | enables, allows, resolves, advances, clarifies, provides |

## Literature Reviews: Enriching Problems, Not Demonstrating Reading

### Student Lit Reviews (Wrong)

"In 1998, Smith argued X. In 2001, Jones responded Y. In 2005, Brown extended..."

This demonstrates you've read the material. It creates stability.

### Professional Lit Reviews (Right)

"In 1998, Smith argued X. *But* in 2001, Jones's findings created tension with this view. Brown's 2005 work *complicates matters further*—if we combine Brown with Smith, we face a contradiction that the field has not resolved."

This *builds the problem* through layers of tension. It makes readers care about your intervention.

## The Writing Process vs. Reading Process Gap

John Tutino worked for years on an article about Mexican revolutionary history. A final reader noted his work "appears controversial"—it challenges the dominant historiographical view.

*He didn't know this until the end.*

What he discovered last became his first sentence: "The interpretation just given challenges an entrenched vision of Mexican history."

### The Lesson

You must use writing to think—there's no other way at expert levels. But the structure that helped you think is usually *wrong* for helping readers read.

What you discover at the end of your process often belongs at the *beginning* of the final text.

## Practical Application for Language Models

When generating prose that aims to inform, persuade, or change understanding:

### Before Writing

1. **Identify the reader community.** Who is this for? What do they currently believe?
2. **Locate the instability.** What's wrong, incomplete, or in tension with their current understanding?
3. **Determine costs/benefits.** Why should they care about this instability?

### While Writing

1. **Open with the problem**, not background. Get to "but" or "however" within the first paragraph.
2. **Use instability language** in the first two paragraphs—but, however, although, inconsistent, tension, challenge.
3. **Signal community** when relevant—widely accepted, established view, scholars argue.
4. **Argue, don't explain.** Anticipate doubts; don't just reveal your reasoning.
5. **Make your thesis a solution** to a problem the reader now perceives.

### During Revision (or Self-Evaluation)

Check the opening paragraphs:
- [ ] Is there a problem located in something readers care about?
- [ ] Does instability language appear within the first paragraph?
- [ ] Are costs or benefits of the instability clear?
- [ ] Is the thesis positioned as a solution, not just a claim?
- [ ] Does the prose assume readers will challenge it (argument), or that readers need to see inside the writer's head (explanation)?

## What This Framework Does NOT Mean

- **Not manipulation.** The goal is genuine value for readers, not tricking them.
- **Not contrarianism.** Challenging the dominant view requires *being right*, not just being different.
- **Not abandoning clarity.** Clarity still matters—but only after value is established.
- **Not one-size-fits-all.** Different communities have different codes. Learn them.

---

## Summary Principles

1. **Writing changes readers' ideas.** It doesn't communicate yours.
2. **Value is determined by readers**, not by the text or writer.
3. **Readers search for instability.** Stability language signals "nothing to see here."
4. **Problems come first.** Your thesis is a solution to a problem readers perceive.
5. **Gap framing is weak.** Error framing is stronger but requires the right code.
6. **Argue, don't explain.** Anticipate what readers doubt.
7. **Learn your community's codes.** The words that signal value are specific.
8. **Your writing process ≠ their reading process.** What you discover last often goes first.
