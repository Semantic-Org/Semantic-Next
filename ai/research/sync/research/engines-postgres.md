# PostgreSQL for a Realtime Client-Side Sync Layer: PG18/PG19 Feature Maturity Assessment (June 2026)

## TL;DR
- **Your two load-bearing bets are in opposite states of readiness.** Failover-slot resume (any-box reconnect after primary failover) is STABLE and production-usable today (PG17+), so the LSN-as-cursor architecture is sound. But the JSONB write-amplification problem — your headline concern — is UNSOLVED and will remain so through your Q1 2027 1.0: PG18 and PG19 ship nothing for partial/in-place TOAST updates, and a one-field change to a 150–200KB JSONB still rewrites the whole value and ships it whole downstream.
- **PG18 (shipped Sept 25, 2025) is your foundation; PG19 (Beta 1 June 4, 2026, GA ~Sept/Oct 2026) is a usable-by-mid-2026 bet only in beta.** PG18 gives you uuidv7(), RETURNING OLD/NEW, virtual generated columns, AIO, and a maturing built-in conflict-detection framework — all directly relevant. PG19's headline win for you is logical replication of sequences (finally) plus on-demand logical decoding without restart, but you cannot build a 1.0 foundation on it; treat PG19 features as STABLE only after GA.
- **Build row-auth in your application layer, not Postgres RLS.** For a stateless fan-out tier tailing logical replication, native RLS doesn't apply to the decoded WAL stream at all, and its connection-pooling gotchas make it a poor fit for your two-gate model. Use RLS only as optional defense-in-depth on the origin database.

## Key Findings

**Cluster 1 — Logical replication as a totally-ordered log:** STABLE and ready. Failover slot synchronization (PG17) lets a consumer resume its LSN cursor against a promoted standby without re-snapshotting; logical decoding on standby (PG16) lets you offload decoding to read replicas. Commit-order/LSN-order caveats are real and well-understood. Sequence replication arrives in PG19 (UNDER DEVELOPMENT, beta now).

**Cluster 2 — CDC / log-as-primitive:** `pg_logical_emit_message()` is STABLE (PG14) and is the strongest under-appreciated primitive for your external-writer backstop — a transactional WAL marker with no table and no write amplification. The 2026 ecosystem favors lightweight, Kafka-free CDC (Sequin, Electric) over Debezium for high-throughput Postgres-as-a-log.

**Cluster 3 — JSONB write amplification:** The critical negative finding. STABLE behavior is hostile to field-granular writes on large documents. No relief in PG18/PG19. Your "scalar keys → columns, nested → JSONB" design is exactly the recommended mitigation, but you must minimize how often the JSONB column itself is rewritten.

**Cluster 4 — Before-images:** REPLICA IDENTITY FULL (STABLE) gives you old values but at real cost. PG18's built-in conflict detection (`track_commit_timestamp`, `retain_dead_tuples`/`retain_conflict_info`) is EXPERIMENTAL-to-STABLE but solves a different problem (multi-master last-write-wins), not your app-level park-then-verify rebase.

**Cluster 5 — Write-path primitives:** uuidv7(), RETURNING OLD/NEW, and virtual generated columns are all STABLE in PG18 and all directly useful.

**Cluster 6 — Per-doc serial apply:** SELECT FOR UPDATE / SKIP LOCKED is STABLE and correct for multi-node serial apply. AIO (PG18) is STABLE but mostly helps read-side throughput, not your small-write+fanout pattern.

**Cluster 7 — Row auth:** RLS is STABLE but a poor architectural fit for a log-tailing fan-out tier. Enforce row auth in the app at two gates.

## Details

### Cluster 1 — Logical replication as a totally-ordered log (cursor = LSN)

**Failover slots / slot synchronization — STABLE (PG17+).** This is the feature that makes your "clients reconnect to any box" design viable across a primary failover. Before PG17, all logical slots lived only on the primary; a failover meant recreating the slot and re-snapshotting from scratch, losing your cursor. PG17 added a slot sync worker (`sync_replication_slots=on`) that synchronizes failover-enabled slots (`CREATE SUBSCRIPTION ... WITH (failover=true)`, or `pg_create_logical_replication_slot(..., failover=true)`) to physical standbys. After promotion the consumer continues from its existing `confirmed_flush_lsn` without missing changes. Requirements: a physical slot between primary and standby, `hot_standby_feedback=on`, `synchronized_standby_slots` configured on the primary to ensure the standby stays ahead of subscribers, and `dbname` in `primary_conninfo`. Verify readiness via `pg_replication_slots.failover_ready`. **Implication:** Your stateless fan-out boxes can tail a synchronized slot and survive failover, but note slot sync is asynchronous — you must confirm the slot synced before failover, or you risk a gap. This is the single feature that most validates your architecture.

**Logical decoding on standby — STABLE (PG16+).** You can create logical slots directly on a hot standby and decode there, offloading the decode CPU cost from the primary. This matters for your fan-out workers: decoding can become a primary-side bottleneck because it is single-threaded per slot. Caveats: slots on the standby get invalidated if `hot_standby_feedback` is off and needed catalog rows are vacuumed away, or if the primary's `wal_level` drops below logical. Use a physical slot between primary and standby to make `hot_standby_feedback` durable across restarts.

**Commit-order vs LSN-order — STABLE behavior, design-critical.** Changes are delivered in commit order, but LSNs within the stream are NOT strictly monotonic per-operation: the "begin" LSN of one transaction can be less than the "commit" LSN of a previously-delivered transaction when transactions ran concurrently. PowerSync documents this exact trap and solves it by maintaining its own server-authoritative monotonic "operation ID" rather than trusting raw LSNs as a total order. **Implication:** Do not assume the LSN you expose to clients as a resumable cursor is globally monotonic at operation granularity — derive a monotonic op-id server-side, as PowerSync does, or anchor your cursor on commit LSN boundaries only.

**Streaming in-progress transactions — STABLE (PG14, parallel since PG16, parallel default in PG18).** `streaming=on` streams large in-progress transactions before commit, bounded by `logical_decoding_work_mem` (default 64MB, added PG13). PG18 makes `streaming=parallel` the default for new subscriptions. Note a TOAST interaction: decoding may still spill to disk if it has decoded a TOAST insert but not the corresponding main-table insert.

**Two-phase commit decoding — STABLE (framework PG14, pgoutput PG15+).** Available via the `two_phase` slot option. Likely irrelevant to your single-origin log model unless you adopt distributed 2PC.

**Slot-induced WAL retention — STABLE hazard, must be managed.** An inactive or slow slot pins every WAL segment from its `restart_lsn` forward, and will fill the primary's disk. `max_slot_wal_keep_size` (PG13+, default -1 = unlimited) is the safety valve: when a slot's retention exceeds the limit the slot is invalidated (`wal_status='lost'`) rather than letting the disk fill — but an invalidated slot forces your consumer to re-snapshot. PG18 adds `idle_replication_slot_timeout` (a time-based counterpart) to invalidate slots inactive for a configurable period. **Implication:** Your "reconnect to any box" resume guarantee is bounded by `max_slot_wal_keep_size`. Set it generously (multiples of hourly WAL rate) but finite, alert on `wal_status='unreserved'`, and design your client protocol to handle the "slot lost, must re-snapshot" path gracefully. Long-running transactions also stall `restart_lsn` advancement even on a healthy consumer.

### Cluster 2 — CDC / change capture

**`pg_logical_emit_message()` — STABLE (PG14).** This is the under-weighted primitive worth leaning into. It writes a message directly into the WAL (transactional or non-transactional), decodable via logical decoding, with no table, no heap tuple, no TOAST, and no dead-tuple/vacuum cost. For your external-writer backstop and transactional-outbox needs it is strictly better than an outbox table: the message commits atomically with the surrounding transaction and never doubles your storage. Gunnar Morling's widely-cited InfoQ writeup ("The Wonders of Postgres Logical Decoding Messages") documents outbox, audit-log, and aggregate-event patterns. The message payload can be a rich aggregate spanning multiple tables — directly relevant to your large-aggregate-document model. Caveat: pgoutput emits these in a binary format you can't inspect with `pg_logical_slot_peek_changes`; the message appears in the logical stream as a dedicated "Logical Decoding Message" with a `transactional` flag.

**pgoutput vs custom plugins — STABLE.** pgoutput is the built-in standard and the only option on managed Postgres (RDS, Cloud SQL, Supabase do not allow custom C plugins like wal2json). For a 1.0 targeting broad deployability, build on pgoutput's logical replication protocol (message formats documented in §54.9) rather than a custom plugin.

**2026 ecosystem best practice.** The market has moved toward lightweight, Kafka-free, Postgres-tuned CDC. **Sequin** (open source, single Docker container, exactly-once, backfills) publishes benchmarks of sustained ~40k operations/sec (40 MB/s) at 55ms average latency from a single replication slot. Per Sequin's own performance docs: "Sequin beats Debezium in a head-to-head comparison on messages per second by 6.8x and on average latency by 4.7x. The highest throughput we were able to consistently achieve with Debezium deployed on AWS MSK Connect was 6k ops/sec. At 10k ops/sec, Debezium's latency grew unbounded." **Electric** (GA 1.0 March 2025) is the closest existing system to your design: it tails logical replication, matches changes against "shapes" (filtered partial replicas with a WHERE clause), appends them to an append-only per-shape log indexed by offset, and fans out via an HTTP protocol behind a CDN. Its v1.1 storage-engine rewrite (Aug 13, 2025, "Electric 1.1: new storage engine with 100x faster writes") delivered "102X faster writes and 73X faster reads on SSD," with read-path latency ~3.8x faster at 1,000 concurrent clients. Electric's cloud benchmarks scale "from 100k to 1 million concurrent clients under a sustained load of 960 writes/minute" with flat memory/latency. Critically for you: **Electric does read-path sync only; writes go through your own API** — exactly your "server half" model. Electric requires `REPLICA IDENTITY FULL` on synced tables and, on PG18+, sets `publish_generated_columns=stored`. PowerSync is the other strong reference (server-authoritative ordering, schemaless replication, client-side SQLite views). Debezium remains the choice when you're already invested in Kafka/Flink. **Implication:** Study Electric's shape-log-by-offset and PowerSync's op-id design directly; they have already solved your fan-out, resume-by-offset, and TOAST-gap problems on the same Postgres primitives.

### Cluster 3 — JSONB for large nested aggregate documents (the headline concern)

**The core problem is STABLE and unmitigated: there is no partial/in-place JSONB update in PostgreSQL, and none is coming in PG18 or PG19.** Updating a single key with `jsonb_set()` or subscripting rewrites the entire JSONB value. For a value above the ~2KB threshold stored in TOAST, every field write means: full detoast → decompress → modify → recompress → write a brand-new TOAST chunk chain + TOAST index entries → new heap tuple → and a full-size WAL record proportional to the whole document, not the changed field. Under MVCC this also creates dead tuples and vacuum pressure proportional to full document size. One production report (SitePoint's JSONB performance guide) describes a notification system that hit WAL amplification at ~500K rows, where each "mark as read" rewrote 4–8KB of JSONB; promoting the three most-updated fields (`last_read_at`, `unread_count`, `badge_count`) to typed columns dropped WAL volume by roughly 80%. As pganalyze's Lukas Fittl puts it, TOAST "wasn't designed for updating a small part of a large value… Postgres has no way to avoid making a full copy of that TOAST value."

**The roadmap is empty here (verified against mailing list / commitfest).** The flagship effort to fix this — Postgres Professional's "Pluggable TOAST" / type-aware `jsonb_toaster` (Commitfest 38/3490, targeted PG16), which explicitly promised "only updated part and appended value is written to WAL" — was **REJECTED**. Nikita Malakhov confirmed on pgsql-hackers: "The Pluggable TOAST was rejected." Andres Freund's verdict: "a lot of complexity, with a (for me) commensurate benefit." A central blocker was that changing the TOAST pointer structure created "very serious issue with logical replication." Robert Haas's related idea (don't store the data length in the TOAST pointer, to allow expansion without rewriting everything) is "being discussed but not currently implemented." Follow-on type-internal work has not produced a PG18/19 patch. **There is no commitfest entry, commit, or release-note line for partial JSONB update, in-place TOAST update, or delta-WAL for large-value updates as of mid-2026.** (The committed "shared detoast" work is a read-side optimization only and does not reduce write amplification.)

**The replication question — also unmitigated, design-critical.** When the JSONB column changes, logical replication ships the FULL new 150–200KB value downstream. The "unchanged-toast-datum" optimization ONLY suppresses transmission of TOASTed columns that did NOT change in that UPDATE. So your field-granular writes pay the full document size in WAL *and* in downstream fan-out bandwidth on every edit. With default REPLICA IDENTITY, an unchanged TOASTed value is sent as a placeholder (Debezium surfaces `__debezium_unavailable_value`); consumers must materialize the last-known value themselves (stateful stream processing — what PowerSync and Electric do).

**REPLICA IDENTITY interaction.** Under REPLICA IDENTITY FULL, the full old row (including TOASTed columns) is always written to WAL for UPDATE/DELETE — useful for before-images but expensive precisely for your large JSONB. There was historically a bug where a TOASTed column in the replica identity could cause updates to silently not replicate when the key didn't change; use a primary key (e.g., your uuidv7 id) as the replica identity rather than putting JSONB in it.

**Newer JSONB surface — STABLE, useful but not a write-amp fix.** JSON_TABLE (PG17) lets you project nested JSONB into relational rows cleanly (replacing nested LATERAL/jsonb_array_elements) — useful for your server-side query/derivation half. JSON_VALUE/JSON_QUERY/JSON_EXISTS (PG17) allow typed extraction. jsonb subscripting (PG14) allows `data['k'] = '...'` update syntax but still rewrites the whole value and always creates missing paths (unlike `jsonb_set`'s `create_if_missing=false`). None of these reduce write amplification.

**TOAST compression — STABLE.** `default_toast_compression` can be `pglz` (default) or `lz4` (PG14+, requires `--with-lz4` build). Per Fujitsu's PostgreSQL Fastware benchmark, LZ4 is dramatically faster: data insertion is "80% faster compared to PGLZ" and compression is "60% to 70% faster," at the cost of ~7% worse compression ratio (pglz averaged 2.23x vs LZ4 2.07x). zstd for TOAST is NOT a core option (only pglz/lz4); zstd exists for WAL full-page-image compression. **Implication:** Switch the JSONB column to `lz4` to cut the CPU cost of the unavoidable full rewrites and detoast-on-read. This is the one cheap, STABLE win available for Cluster 3. Consider `EXTERNAL` storage to skip compression if your documents are poorly compressible and you do substring reads, but it does not help updates.

**Net design implication:** The storage floor for field-granular writes to large JSONB is set by full-value rewrite + full-value WAL + full-value fan-out. This is a hard constraint for your 1.0. Mitigations, all app-side: (1) keep more scalar keys as native columns so the JSONB column is rewritten less often; (2) split a large aggregate into multiple smaller JSONB columns or sub-rows so a write rewrites only the relevant slice; (3) batch/coalesce field writes per document to amortize the rewrite; (4) use lz4. Do not architect as if a cheap partial JSONB update will arrive before 1.0 — it won't.

### Cluster 4 — Before-images for park-then-verify

**REPLICA IDENTITY FULL — STABLE.** Logs the entire old row into WAL on UPDATE/DELETE, giving you per-row before-images in the logical stream. Cost: more WAL, more CPU on the publisher, more network to subscribers — and it's worst exactly where you feel it most (large JSONB rows). Since PG16, the subscriber side can use an index to find rows under REPLICA IDENTITY FULL, easing one historical performance cliff. **Implication:** REPLICA IDENTITY FULL would hand you base/before values per written path "for free" in the stream, but at the large-JSONB write-amp cost you're already fighting. For park-then-verify, prefer capturing before-images on your owned write path (see RETURNING OLD/NEW below) rather than turning on FULL globally.

**PG18 built-in conflict detection — EXPERIMENTAL-to-STABLE, but a different problem.** PG18 ships a real conflict-detection framework: `track_commit_timestamp`, the `retain_conflict_info`/retain-dead-tuples machinery (reserved slot `pg_conflict_detection`), and detection of conflict types `insert_exists`, `update_origin_differs`, `update_missing`, `update_deleted`, `delete_origin_differs`, `delete_missing`, and `multiple_unique_conflicts`, with statistics in `pg_stat_subscription_stats`. Resolution policies (`last_update_wins`, `keep_local`, etc.) are on the community roadmap toward a `conflict_resolver` subscription parameter (UNDER DEVELOPMENT). **Implication:** This is built for multi-master / active-active Postgres-to-Postgres replication and last-write-wins resolution. It does NOT implement your application-level park-then-verify rebase against per-path base values. It can supplement observability (detecting that a subscriber row diverged) but cannot replace your app-level conflict model. Don't couple your 1.0 to it.

### Cluster 5 — Write-path primitives (all STABLE in PG18)

**uuidv7() — STABLE (PG18).** Native timestamp-ordered UUIDs; `uuidv4()` added as an alias for `gen_random_uuid()`. Time-ordering gives B-tree locality (fewer page splits) versus random v4, which matters for your id-addressed array paths and ordering. Accepts an interval argument to shift the embedded timestamp. **Implication:** Use uuidv7() for document/operation ids — you get global uniqueness plus an implicit creation-time order usable as a coarse secondary sort, without a central sequence. Note: before PG18 you generated these in-app; if you must run on PG17 or a managed service lacking PG18, keep your app-side generator.

**RETURNING OLD/NEW — STABLE (PG18).** INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE/MERGE can return both `old.col` and `new.col` (aliases renamable) in a single statement. This is directly aligned with your "emit before/after deltas off the owned-write path" requirement: your server-half write can capture the before-image and after-image atomically in one round trip, no separate SELECT, no trigger, no race window. `(old IS NULL)` even distinguishes insert-vs-update in an upsert. **Implication:** This is the right primitive for park-then-verify before-image capture on the owned path — far cheaper and cleaner than REPLICA IDENTITY FULL. Build your delta emission on it. Caveat: PG18-only, so it's a true dependency to confirm in your deployment matrix.

**Virtual generated columns — STABLE (PG18, now the default for generated columns).** Computed on read, no storage, no write cost (vs STORED, computed and stored on write). Important replication caveat: STORED generated columns CAN now be logically replicated (PG18); VIRTUAL columns are NOT replicated (computed on read at each end). **Implication:** The DB can own some derivations cheaply with VIRTUAL columns for server-side query convenience, but anything a downstream consumer must see over logical replication needs to be STORED (and STORED adds write cost). For your model, keep derivations app-side or as VIRTUAL for local query; don't expect VIRTUAL to appear in the change stream.

### Cluster 6 — Per-doc serial apply via row locks

**SELECT FOR UPDATE / SKIP LOCKED / NOWAIT — STABLE (SKIP LOCKED since 9.5).** The canonical multi-node-correct queue: `SELECT ... WHERE ... ORDER BY ... LIMIT 1 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`. SKIP LOCKED lets each node grab a different available document without blocking, the lock is bound to the transaction (crash → auto-rollback → row reverts to claimable), and this is the right primitive for your per-document serial apply. NOWAIT fails fast instead of skipping, for when you want to detect contention. **Hot-document contention:** for a single hot document edited by many operators, all your serial-apply workers will contend on that one row — SKIP LOCKED makes them skip rather than queue, which is correct, but throughput on that document is inherently serial (which is your design intent). Watch for MultiXact SLRU contention and WAL volume if hot documents get extremely high concurrency; every lock/claim/complete is WAL-logged. Index the claim predicate (e.g., partial index on pending state) so the lock touches the minimum rows.

**Lock observability — STABLE, improving.** `pg_stat_activity` (wait_event_type/wait_event), `pg_locks`, and `pg_blocking_pids()` cover blocked-on-whom. PG19 (UNDER DEVELOPMENT, beta) adds first-class system views for lock contention and `pg_stat_recovery` for standby state.

**AIO (PG18) — STABLE, limited relevance.** The new asynchronous I/O subsystem (`io_method` = `worker` | `io_uring` | `sync`) issues multiple I/O requests concurrently, with demonstrated up to ~3x gains on sequential scans, bitmap heap scans, and vacuum. **Implication:** AIO primarily helps read-heavy/scan/vacuum throughput. Your workload is high-frequency small writes plus delta fan-out — AIO will help your background vacuum keep up with the dead-tuple churn from JSONB rewrites (a real secondary benefit given Cluster 3), and help large scans, but it does not directly accelerate the small-write commit path. Treat it as helpful operational headroom, not a core lever.

### Cluster 7 — Row-level authentication/authorization

**RLS — STABLE, but the wrong layer for your fan-out tier.** PostgreSQL RLS (policies with USING for read/visibility and WITH CHECK for write, permissive vs restrictive, FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY for table owners) is mature and a genuinely good fit for multi-tenant isolation *inside a directly-queried database*. Best practices in 2026: index your policy predicate column (an unindexed policy becomes a full scan on every query); always set FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY (table owners and superusers bypass RLS by default — the most common silent-failure gotcha, where you test as owner and conclude policies work when they never ran); keep policy expressions LEAKPROOF-friendly so they push down to index scans; pass identity via `current_setting('app.xxx')` session GUCs.

**The connection-pooling gotcha — STABLE, severe.** Behind PgBouncer in transaction-pooling mode (the standard production setup), a connection is shared across clients. You MUST use `SET LOCAL` (transaction-scoped, auto-resets at COMMIT) inside an explicit transaction, never `SET` (session-scoped) — otherwise the next client to borrow that pooled connection inherits the previous tenant's context and you leak data across tenants. Statement-pooling mode breaks SET/SET LOCAL entirely. This is the classic, dangerous footgun.

**Why it's a poor fit for your architecture, and what to do instead.** Your auth is row/document-granularity only (good — no JSONB-subtree ACLs to worry about). But three things argue against coupling to native RLS: (1) RLS applies to SQL queries against the origin database — it does **nothing** for changes decoded off the logical replication stream that your stateless fan-out boxes tail; the WAL decode bypasses RLS entirely. (2) Your fan-out tier holds long-lived replication connections, not pooled per-request transactions, so the SET LOCAL model doesn't map. (3) Electric, the closest production analog, deliberately does NOT use RLS — it puts an authorization proxy in front of the sync service and gates shape subscriptions there. **Recommendation:** Implement your two app-level gates — read auth at channel subscribe (does this client get this row/shape?) and write auth in the operation's server half (may this client perform this write?) — in application code. This maps cleanly onto your channel-selector + permission-gate model and is the same pattern Electric and PowerSync use. Use Postgres RLS only as optional defense-in-depth on the origin DB for any direct (non-sync) query paths, with FORCE RLS and SET LOCAL discipline. No material RLS changes in PG18/PG19 affect this conclusion.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Lock your version target now.** Build the 1.0 foundation on **PostgreSQL 18** (STABLE since Sept 25, 2025; 18.1 Nov 2025, 18.3 Feb 2026 — mature by mid-2026). It gives you uuidv7(), RETURNING OLD/NEW, virtual generated columns, AIO, parallel-streaming-by-default, and built-in conflict-detection observability. Do NOT make PG19 features load-bearing for 1.0: PG19 GA is ~Sept/Oct 2026, leaving no margin before Q1 2027 for it to prove stable. Per the PG19 Beta 1 release notes (June 4, 2026), sequence replication arrives via `CREATE PUBLICATION ... FOR ALL SEQUENCES` plus `ALTER SUBSCRIPTION ... REFRESH SEQUENCES` (a `sequencesync` worker) — note sequences sync only on REFRESH, not continuously — so even when GA it is not a live-sequence-replication feature. Treat anything PG19-only as a post-1.0 enhancement.

**Stage 1 — Validate the cursor/resume path early.** Prototype failover-slot sync (PG17+) end-to-end: tail a synchronized slot from a fan-out box, kill the primary, promote the standby, confirm cursor resume with no re-snapshot. Implement a server-side monotonic op-id (à la PowerSync) rather than exposing raw LSNs as the client cursor. Set `max_slot_wal_keep_size` finite-but-generous and build the "slot lost → re-snapshot" client path from day one. Benchmark logical-decoding-on-standby to decide whether decode runs on the primary or replicas.

**Stage 2 — De-risk JSONB write amplification with a hard benchmark.** This is your highest-risk area. Build a representative 150–200KB document and measure WAL bytes and downstream fan-out bytes for a single-field write under: (a) full JSONB in one column, (b) hot scalars promoted to native columns, (c) the aggregate split into N smaller JSONB columns/sub-rows, all with `lz4`. Use the results to set your storage model and your write-coalescing strategy. Threshold that changes the design: if per-edit WAL/fan-out exceeds your latency or cost budget at expected edit frequency (10-100 concurrent writers, field-granular, over days/months), you must split documents more aggressively or coalesce writes — do not wait for a Postgres fix.

**Stage 3 — Build before-images and deltas on the owned write path.** Use RETURNING OLD/NEW (PG18) in the server-half write to capture base/after values per path atomically for park-then-verify. Avoid REPLICA IDENTITY FULL globally; if you need before-images from the replication stream for the external-writer backstop, scope FULL to small tables only, never the large-JSONB table. Use `pg_logical_emit_message()` for the transactional-outbox/marker primitive.

**Stage 4 — Auth in the app, two gates.** Read-auth at channel subscribe, write-auth in the operation's server half. Keep RLS optional/defense-in-depth on the origin DB with FORCE RLS + SET LOCAL. Don't couple sync-path auth to RLS.

**Stage 5 — Operational guardrails.** Switch the JSONB column to `lz4`. Tune autovacuum aggressively for the high-churn document table (JSONB rewrites generate dead tuples proportional to document size); AIO will help vacuum keep up. Alert on slot `wal_status`, inactive slots, and `restart_lsn` lag. Index every SKIP LOCKED claim predicate.

## Caveats
- **PG19 specifics are beta and may change before GA.** Sequence replication, `CREATE PUBLICATION ... EXCEPT`, on-demand logical decoding / `effective_wal_level`, `WAIT FOR LSN`, REPACK CONCURRENTLY, and parallel autovacuum are confirmed in Beta 1 (June 4, 2026) but are UNDER DEVELOPMENT until GA (~Sept/Oct 2026). Anything not at least in beta by mid-2026 is a bet, not a foundation — and even beta features carry GA-slippage and behavior-change risk against a Q1 2027 1.0.
- **The JSONB partial-update limitation is the firmest negative finding in this report.** It is corroborated across PostgreSQL docs, core-developer mailing-list threads (Pluggable TOAST rejection), and multiple independent engineering blogs. Architect around it as a permanent constraint for your release horizon.
- **Vendor CDC benchmarks (Sequin vs Debezium; Electric's 102x writes) are self-published** and run in the vendor's chosen environment; treat the specific multipliers as directional, not independently verified. The qualitative trend (lightweight Postgres-tuned CDC outperforming Kafka-based Debezium at high throughput) is broadly consistent across sources.
- **Built-in conflict detection (PG18) and its resolution roadmap target active-active Postgres**, not app-level rebase. Mapping it onto your park-then-verify model would be a misfit; this report recommends against it for 1.0.
- **Managed-Postgres constraints:** RDS/Cloud SQL/Supabase restrict you to pgoutput (no custom C plugins like wal2json) and gate logical replication behind provider flags; confirm `wal_level=logical`, failover-slot support, and PG18 availability on your target platform before committing.
